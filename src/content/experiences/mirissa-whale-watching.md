## What you might see

The south coast has a narrow continental shelf, so deep water lies only a short run offshore. That is where the whales feed, within reach of a morning boat trip.

- **Blue whales.** The main reason most people go. In a 2017 scientific survey off Mirissa, they made up the large majority of whale encounters. You usually see a tall blow, a long blue-grey back, and sometimes the tail lifting as the whale dives.
- **Sperm whales.** Seen regularly, sometimes in small groups. They dive for long periods, so a sighting can mean waiting, then a few minutes at the surface.
- **Dolphins.** Spinner dolphins often travel in large, fast-moving groups. Bottlenose dolphins are also seen.
- **Occasional visitors.** Bryde’s and Omura’s whales, short-finned pilot whales and, rarely, killer whales.

Operators’ own records suggest whales are found on most trips in the main months, but this is wild animal behaviour and the sea. Some mornings bring a distant blow or nothing at all, and no trip can promise a sighting.

## Season and sea conditions

Whales live in Sri Lankan waters year-round. What limits the season at Mirissa is the sea. From about **November to April**, the north-east monsoon keeps the south coast relatively sheltered, and boats go out most days. From about **May to October**, the south-west monsoon brings heavy swell, and few trips run. In those months, [Trincomalee](/places/trincomalee/) on the east coast has its own whale-watching season.

Even in season, a long swell can roll in from the south. Mornings are often calmer than later in the day. The regulations say boats must not go out in bad weather and must head back if it turns, so a trip can be cancelled at short notice. This is a good reason to leave a spare morning in your plans.

## What a trip involves

Most trips leave the harbour between about 6 and 7 am, after a safety briefing. Boats range from small converted fishing boats to double-decker vessels carrying dozens of passengers. The run out to deep water can take an hour or more, then the crew scans for blows. A typical trip spends 3–5 hours at sea and returns by late morning or midday, longer if the search goes on.

Under the regulations, each trip also needs a wildlife permit, issued at the departure point. Ask whether it is included in the price.

## Choosing a responsible boat

Mirissa’s popularity has brought crowding. Visitors have described a dozen or more boats around a single whale, and reports describe some boats getting far closer than the rules allow. The 2012 sea-mammal regulations require boats to:

- slow down gradually once whales are seen, and stop at about 400 m
- switch off the engine 100 m from a whale and never go closer, keeping at least 50 m from dolphins
- avoid sudden turns or changes of speed, never sit in front of or behind the animals, and never block their path
- never use food, lights or sounds to draw animals closer
- carry a registered guide trained by the wildlife department, and no alcohol on board.

When choosing a boat, ask whether the vessel is licensed by the Department of Wildlife Conservation, and whether life jackets are provided for everyone, including children. Ask how the crew approaches whales, too. Boats that offer swimming with whales are breaking the rules: passengers may not enter the water without special approval from the wildlife department. On board, you can ask the crew to hold back, or to leave when too many boats gather.

## Seasickness and comfort

If you are prone to seasickness, ask a pharmacist about a remedy before the trip and take it as directed. The NHS advises sitting where there is least movement (on a boat, the middle and lower deck), looking at the horizon rather than at screens or the waves, getting fresh air, and avoiding heavy meals and alcohol before travelling. Eat a light breakfast and drink water.

Boats can bang hard through the swell, so check with your doctor first if you are pregnant or have back or heart problems. Young children may find several hours at sea tiring.

## What to bring

- Sunscreen, a hat and sunglasses; shade on deck can be limited.
- A light layer or windproof jacket for the early start.
- Drinking water and a seasickness remedy if you use one.
- A camera with a zoom lens, or binoculars, since boats stay well back.
- A waterproof bag for your phone and camera.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are whale sightings guaranteed?

No. Whales are seen on many trips in season, but some mornings bring only distant views or none. Allow a spare morning if seeing a blue whale matters to you.

### How long does a whale-watching trip take?

Usually 3–5 hours at sea, leaving between about 6 and 7 am and returning by late morning or midday. Searches can take longer.

### Can I swim with the whales or dolphins?

No. Sri Lanka’s sea-mammal regulations do not let passengers into the water without special approval from the wildlife department, and boats must keep their distance.

### Is whale watching suitable for children?

Older children who cope with boats often enjoy it. The early start, several hours at sea and possible seasickness make it hard for young children. Check that the boat has life jackets in children’s sizes.

### What happens if the sea is too rough?

Boats are not allowed out in bad weather, and trips are cancelled or cut short when the sea turns. Ask about the rebooking and refund policy before you book.
