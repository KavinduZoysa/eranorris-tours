## Why the relic matters

According to tradition, the tooth was taken from the Buddha’s funeral pyre and reached Sri Lanka in the 4th century AD, hidden in the hair of a princess. From then on it became the island’s great symbol of kingship. Whoever held the relic held the right to rule, and the capital moved with it — from Anuradhapura to Polonnaruwa and on through later kingdoms. It came to Kandy in the late 16th century, and the city has kept it ever since.

The relic itself is never shown. It rests in a set of nested golden caskets in an inner chamber that only a few custodians enter. What visitors share in is the devotion around it: the drums, the flowers and the steady stream of worshippers.

## What you will see

The temple stands inside the old royal palace grounds, behind a moat. The parts most visitors notice:

- **The octagon (Pattirippuwa).** The tiered pavilion overlooking the lake was added by the last king of Kandy as a place to address his subjects. It now holds a library of palm-leaf manuscripts.
- **The shrine rooms.** Stairs lead up to the upper floor, where worshippers gather before golden doors to the relic chamber. The ceilings and carvings are richly painted and gilded. It can be crowded and very busy around the ceremonies.
- **The newer shrine hall and museums.** Elsewhere in the complex, a later hall holds rows of seated Buddha images and a museum of gifts and regalia. The ticket also covers several museums in the complex. The temple lists the Dalada Museum, the World Buddhist Museum and the Tusker Museum, and the old audience hall stands nearby.

## The ceremonies

Worship follows a daily rhythm. Three times a day — early morning, mid-morning and evening — drummers play, offerings are carried in, and the doors of the inner chamber open for devotees. These are the most atmospheric times to be there, and also the busiest. On Wednesday mornings there is an additional ritual, the herbal bathing of the relic.

Exact ceremony times are published on the temple’s visitor FAQ. If you want a quieter visit, go between the ceremonies. If you want the drums and the crowds, arrive a little before one.

## Visiting respectfully

- **Clothing.** Cover your shoulders and knees. The temple turns away visitors in shorts, sleeveless tops or revealing clothing. Suitable clothing is available at the entrances, but it is simpler to arrive dressed for it.
- **Shoes and bags.** Shoes are left at a reception booth, with a separate one for foreign visitors. You get a token in return, and everyone passes a security check. Leave plastic bags and anything bulky behind; pets are not allowed.
- **Tickets.** Foreign visitors buy tickets from the machines on site. The temple warns that tickets sold by anyone else are not valid.
- **Inside.** Walk quietly, keep to the flow of worshippers, and don’t pose for photos with your back to Buddha images. Ask before photographing people at prayer, and follow any signs or staff instructions about photography.
- **Access.** Visitors with limited mobility can contact the temple administration in advance about transport from the main entrance and use of the lift. The number is on the visitor FAQ.

## Timing your visit

An hour is enough to see the shrine and the octagon. Two hours lets you take in the museums and a ceremony without rushing. Many travellers pair the temple with a walk around the lake, or with [Peradeniya’s botanic gardens](/places/kandy/) on the same day. On full-moon (poya) days and holidays the temple is especially busy with pilgrims.

Once a year, usually in July or August, the relic’s casket leads the **Esala Perahera**. These are nights of torch-lit processions through Kandy, with dancers, drummers and decorated elephants. The temple publishes the dates; see the [Kandy guide](/places/kandy/) for planning around it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you see the tooth relic itself?

No. The relic stays in nested golden caskets inside a closed chamber. During the daily ceremonies the doors of the chamber open, and worshippers file past to pay their respects.

### When are the ceremonies?

There are three every day: early in the morning, mid-morning and in the evening. The temple’s visitor FAQ gives the current times.

### Do I need to buy tickets in advance?

There is no need. Foreign visitors buy tickets at the machines on site, and the temple says tickets sold any other way are not valid. Prices are listed on the visitor FAQ and change from time to time.

### Is the temple accessible for visitors with limited mobility?

The temple can arrange transport from the main entrance and use of a lift for visitors with special needs. Contact the administration office in advance; the details are on the visitor FAQ.

### Can I visit during the Esala Perahera?

Yes, but expect very large crowds, closed streets and heavy security around the temple. Book accommodation in Kandy well ahead, and check the temple’s published schedule for the procession nights.
