## A plateau at the top of the island

Horton Plains is the highest plateau in Sri Lanka, a rolling sweep of tussock grassland and cloud forest at roughly 2,100–2,300 m. Mist drifts across it on most days, the average temperature is only about 13 °C, and ground frost is common early in the year. Streams rising here feed three of the island’s great rivers, the Mahaweli, the Kelani and the Walawe.

The plains were named in 1834 after Sir Robert Wilmot-Horton, then governor of Ceylon, and became a hunting ground for colonial officials. Farr Inn, a former hunting lodge, is now the visitor centre. The area became a wildlife sanctuary in 1969 and a national park in 1988, managed by the Department of Wildlife Conservation. Since 2010 it has been one of the three parts of UNESCO’s Central Highlands of Sri Lanka World Heritage site, with the Peak Wilderness around [Adam’s Peak](/places/adams-peak/) and the Knuckles range.

## The World’s End loop

Most visitors walk a single circuit of about 9 km that starts and ends near the visitor centre. It takes around three hours at an easy pace. The path is mostly level, but rocky, rooty and muddy in places.

- **Mini World’s End** comes first, a viewpoint where the plateau begins to fall away.
- **World’s End** is the reason most people come. Here the edge of the plateau drops in a sheer escarpment of roughly 870–880 m. On a clear morning you look south over tea villages and the lowlands, and on the best days towards the coast.
- **Baker’s Falls**, about 20 m high on the Belihul Oya, is named after Samuel Baker, the explorer and hunter who founded the settlement at Nuwara Eliya. The path down to its viewpoint is steep and slippery when wet.

The loop then crosses open grassland back to the start.

### Safety at the edge

There are no railings at World’s End, and the rock at the lip can crumble. Visitors have fallen to their deaths here, including a tourist taking a photograph in 2018. Stay well back from the edge, keep children close, and don’t climb out onto outcrops for pictures. In mist, the drop can appear with little warning.

## When to go

- **Time of day.** Sri Lanka Tourism advises being there between about 6 and 10 am. Cloud usually rises up the escarpment by mid-morning and can hide the view completely, so most people leave Nuwara Eliya before dawn.
- **Time of year.** The view is most often clear from about January to March. Mist and rain are frequent from about April to September, and October to December is often wet too; the plateau gets over 2,000 mm of rain a year.
- **Crowds and closures.** Weekends, poya (full moon) days and public holidays are busiest. The park can also close in severe weather, as it did for a time during Cyclone Ditwah in late 2025.

## Permits and park rules

Entry needs a permit from the Department of Wildlife Conservation, charged per visitor and per vehicle. You can reserve a day-visitor e-permit on the department’s website (a printed receipt with its QR code is easiest to show) or buy one at the park. The department sets fees, opening hours and walking times, and they change, so check its pages before you go.

- **No polythene.** Plastic bags and wrappers are banned, after sambar deer died from eating litter. Bags may be checked, and whatever you carry in should leave with you.
- **Stay on the trail.** The grassland and marshes are fragile, and short cuts widen the eroded tracks.
- **Leave the animals alone.** Don’t feed or approach the deer.
- **Not a through route.** Since December 2025 the roads across the plains have again been open only to ticketed park visitors.

## Wildlife and plants

Sambar deer are the animal you are most likely to see, often grazing on the grassland early in the morning. The park is an Important Bird Area with several endemic species, among them the Sri Lanka white-eye and the Sri Lanka wood pigeon, so binoculars are worth carrying. Leopards live here but are seldom seen, and the Horton Plains slender loris, a nocturnal primate first photographed here in 2010, is almost never seen by day visitors. No sighting is guaranteed.

The cloud forest is low, wind-bent and hung with moss, with red-flowering rhododendron among the trees. In places you will pass stands of dead trees: forest dieback was first noted here in the 1940s. The IUCN, which advises UNESCO, also names invasive plants and crowding at peak times among the pressures on the park.

## Getting there

- **From [Nuwara Eliya](/places/nuwara-eliya/).** About 32 km via Ambewela and Pattipola, usually around 1–1.5 hours on narrow hill roads.
- **From Haputale or Ella.** About 38 km from Haputale via Ohiya, and roughly 2–2.5 hours from [Ella](/places/ella/). Many routes fit the walk into the morning of the drive between Nuwara Eliya and Ella.
- **By train.** Pattipola and Ohiya stations are on the Nanu Oya–Badulla section of the hill-country line, which was running in early November 2026. Ohiya is about 10 km by road from the park. Check Sri Lanka Railways for current times.

## What to bring

Bring warm layers for the cold dawn, and a hat and sunscreen for later, as the plateau warms quickly once the sun is up. Add a rain jacket, shoes with grip, water in a refillable bottle and snacks in a reusable box. The rocky path and steps make the loop unsuitable for wheelchairs and pushchairs.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does the World’s End walk take?

About three hours for the 9 km loop at an easy pace, plus time at the viewpoints. From Nuwara Eliya, allow most of a morning including the drive.

### What time should I arrive?

As early as the park allows. The view from World’s End is usually clearest between about 6 and 10 am, before the cloud rises.

### Is the walk difficult?

Not technically. It is mostly level but uneven, with muddy stretches and a steep, slippery path to Baker’s Falls. Most reasonably fit walkers manage it, though the cold and the altitude can make it feel harder.

### Do I need a guide?

No. The loop is well used and easy to follow, and most visitors walk it on their own. A naturalist can help with birds and plants.

### Can I visit Horton Plains from Ella?

Yes, but it makes a long morning: roughly 2–2.5 hours each way via Haputale and Ohiya, leaving well before dawn. It is easier from Nuwara Eliya, or on the way between the two.
