## Why visit Udawalawe

Udawalawe National Park lies at the southern foot of the hill country, where Sri Lanka’s wet and dry zones meet. It was declared in 1972, partly to protect the catchment of the new Udawalawe reservoir on the Walawe River and partly to give a home to animals displaced when the valley was flooded. The park covers about 308 square kilometres. The reservoir sits at its centre, with the hills rising to the north.

Much of the land is open grassland and low scrub, with stands of dead trees left from old farmland. It can look bare in the dry season, but the open ground makes animals easy to see. The draw is elephants: estimates of the population run from about 250 to several hundred animals, and family herds are seen in every season.

## What you are likely to see

- **Elephants.** Family groups with young calves, lone bulls, and herds drinking or bathing at the reservoir. They are seen on most drives, but they are wild animals and no sighting is guaranteed.
- **Other mammals.** Water buffalo, spotted deer, sambar, wild boar, golden jackals, mongooses, toque macaques and grey langurs, with mugger crocodiles in the reservoir.
- **Birds.** About 180 species have been recorded. Look for eagles and other birds of prey over the grassland, and painted storks and spot-billed pelicans at the water’s edge. Migrants add to the numbers from about October to April.
- **Rarities.** Leopards and sloth bears live in the park but are seldom seen. Go to [Yala](/places/yala/) if leopards are a priority.

## Planning a safari

Visitors tour the park in safari jeeps, which start from the main entrance on the Thanamalwila road. Permits are issued by the Department of Wildlife Conservation, at the gate or online. The park is open from around dawn to dusk; check the department for current hours.

A drive usually lasts about 3–4 hours. Early morning, from opening time until mid-morning, and late afternoon until closing are cooler and often better for elephants on the move. Middle-of-the-day drives are hot and glaring. The tracks are dusty, so bring a hat, sunscreen, water and binoculars.

## The Elephant Transit Home

The Elephant Transit Home, beside the main road a few kilometres west of the park entrance, is a managed rehabilitation facility run by the Department of Wildlife Conservation since 1995. It takes in orphaned and abandoned elephant calves and raises them with limited human contact. When they are about five years old, it releases them into protected areas. Well over a hundred elephants have been released since the late 1990s, including six in 2025.

Visitors watch the calves come in for milk at set feeding times, from a viewing area set back from the animals. You cannot touch or feed them, and between feeds there is often little to see. Feeding times differ between sources, so check them locally on the day. These are young elephants in managed care, not a wild encounter, so you need the national park itself to see elephants living freely.

## Responsible viewing

- **Don’t feed elephants.** For years, some elephants have waited at the park fence along the reservoir road for food from passing vehicles. Feeding wild animals is against the rules. It is also dangerous, and it teaches elephants to approach people.
- **Give them space.** Ask your safari driver to keep a good distance, especially from mothers with calves. Never let the jeep come between a calf and its herd, or block an elephant’s path. If several jeeps have already gathered around a herd, hold back or move on.
- **Read the signs.** Head-shaking, spread ears or a step towards the jeep are common warnings. Back off quietly.
- **Stay in the vehicle** and keep noise and litter to yourself.

## Around Udawalawe

Most people come only for the park, but a few places nearby reward a longer stay or a different route:

- **Maduwanwela Walawwa.** Roughly an hour west, beyond Embilipitiya, this rambling manor house of a local chieftain family dates back to the 1700s and was enlarged until 1905. It once had more than a hundred rooms around 21 courtyards; fewer than half survive. The Department of Archaeology runs it as a museum, and few tourists find it. Check that it is open before you go.
- **Sinharaja Forest Reserve.** The southern edge of this UNESCO-listed rainforest, around Deniyaya, is about 2–2.5 hours away via Embilipitiya. That is a long return trip; it works better as a night of its own on the way to or from the south coast.
- **Lunugamvehera National Park**, near Thanamalwila on the road to Yala, protects a corridor that elephants use between the two parks, and sees few jeeps.

## How long to stay

**Half a day** works as a stop between the south coast and the hills: an early-morning drive, then the Transit Home at a feeding time, then on to the next stop. **One night** is more relaxed. Arrive for an afternoon drive, stay locally, and add the Transit Home the next morning before moving on.

A typical overnight stop:

- **Afternoon:** arrive and take a late-afternoon drive until the park closes.
- **Evening:** a quiet night near the park.
- **Next morning:** the Elephant Transit Home at a morning feeding, then the drive to Ella or the coast.

## Best time to visit

Udawalawe is open all year, and elephants can be seen in any month. It has no regular annual closure like Yala’s, though the wildlife department can close parks at short notice. Rain falls mainly from about October to January and again in March to May. After rain the grassland turns green and temporary pools appear. In dry spells, animals stay closer to the reservoir and the remaining water.

## Getting there and moving on

- **From the airport.** About 3.5–4.5 hours by road, either by the Southern Expressway and then inland, or through Ratnapura. The time depends on the route and on traffic around Colombo.
- **Into the hills.** [Ella](/places/ella/) is about 2–2.5 hours north-east via Wellawaya.
- **To Yala.** Tissamaharama, the base for [Yala](/places/yala/), is about 2 hours east.
- **To the coast.** [Mirissa](/places/mirissa/) is about 2–2.5 hours south, and [Galle](/places/galle/) about 2.5–3 hours, partly on the Southern Expressway.

In a private tour you add Udawalawe to your route on the map, and your driver gets you to the park. The drive times shown in the planner are rough estimates, not road-tested figures.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Udawalawe or Yala better for elephants?

For elephants, Udawalawe is usually the more reliable choice, because herds live there year-round and the open land makes them easy to see. Yala is the better choice for leopards. Neither can guarantee a sighting.

### Can I see elephants at Udawalawe all year?

Yes. Elephants live in the park in every season, and the park has no regular annual closure. Where they gather changes with rain and water levels.

### Is the Elephant Transit Home the same as a safari?

No. It is a managed facility where orphaned calves are raised for release, and you watch them from a viewing area at feeding times. To see wild elephants, take a drive in the national park.

### Should I stop to feed the elephants by the roadside?

No. Feeding wild elephants is against the rules and dangerous, both for you and for the elephants, which learn to approach traffic. Watch from the car and keep moving.

### Can I visit Udawalawe on the way to Ella?

Yes. It lies on a common route between the south coast and Ella. A morning drive and the Transit Home fit into half a day, followed by about 2–2.5 hours’ drive to Ella.
