## Why visit Yala

Yala covers nearly 1,000 square kilometres of the dry south-east, across the Southern and Uva provinces. The land is low and hot: thorn scrub and dry forest broken by grassland, shallow lakes, coastal lagoons, dunes and bare rock outcrops. It was declared a reserve in 1900 and became one of Sri Lanka’s first national parks in 1938.

It is best known for leopards. Block I, the most visited part of the park, has been estimated to hold at least one leopard per square kilometre, among the highest densities recorded. Many of these animals are used to vehicles, so they are often seen in the open. That reputation also brings crowds, and planning around them makes a real difference to your visit.

## How the park is divided

The park is split into blocks, which the wildlife department now often calls zones. Most visitors enter **Block I** through the Palatupana gate near Tissamaharama, or through Katagamuwa in the north near Kataragama. Block II adjoins it.

The **other zones**, entered from Galge and Lunugamvehera further north, are larger and much quieter. Wildlife there is less used to jeeps and harder to see, but you share sightings with far fewer vehicles. The authorities have said they want to spread visitors more evenly across the park.

## What you might see

- **Leopards.** Seen on many drives in Block I, but never guaranteed. Some visitors miss them on several drives in a row.
- **Elephants.** Seen regularly, though their numbers in the park change with the seasons. For large herds, [Udawalawe](/places/udawalawe/) is more reliable.
- **Sloth bears.** Shy and seldom seen, except roughly May to July, when they come out to feed on the fruit of palu trees.
- **Everyday sightings.** Spotted deer, sambar, wild boar, water buffalo, grey langurs, mugger crocodiles and monitor lizards.
- **Birds.** More than 200 species, from peafowl and bee-eaters to painted storks and eagles, with migrant birds from about October to April.

Inside the park, Sithulpawwa is an ancient rock monastery still used by pilgrims; dress as you would for any temple. At Patanangala, on the coast, a memorial marks the 2004 tsunami, which struck this shore.

## Planning a safari

Ordinary cars and vans do not go on safari. Visitors enter the park in registered safari jeeps, and since 2024 only licensed jeeps and drivers have been allowed into Block I. Park permits are issued by the Department of Wildlife Conservation, at the gate or online, and each park has a daily limit.

The park is open from around dawn to dusk, and every vehicle must be out by closing time; the wildlife department publishes the current hours. A **morning drive** usually starts at opening time and returns by late morning. An **afternoon drive** runs from mid-afternoon until closing. A **full day** stays in the park through the midday heat, with stops only at designated places.

Animals are most active early and late, and the morning light is softer for photographs. Bring a hat, sunscreen, water, a scarf against the dust, and binoculars.

## Responsible safari

Block I can be very busy, and crowds of jeeps around a leopard have become a well-reported problem. The way you behave in the jeep counts:

- Tell your safari driver you don’t want to speed or race to sightings. Losing a sighting is better than causing an accident.
- Stay in the jeep except at the designated stops, and keep your voice down near animals.
- Never feed wildlife or leave litter, and don’t play calls or music to attract animals.
- Give elephants space and a clear way past; don’t let the jeep block their path.
- Respect closures and the exit times. They exist to protect the park.

## When to go and park closures

Yala gets most of its rain from the north-east monsoon, roughly October to January. From about February the land dries out, and by August and September water is scarce and animals gather at the remaining waterholes. That makes them easier to find, and it also puts the park under the most stress.

Because of this, the wildlife department usually closes **Blocks I and II for several weeks around September and October**. The dates change every year: in 2026 they were closed from 14 September to 15 October, while the other zones stayed open. Check the department’s announcements before planning a visit in those months.

## How long to stay

**One night** allows an afternoon drive on arrival and a morning drive the next day. **Two nights** add a second morning in the park, a full day, or time for the places nearby. Two drives give you a much better chance of seeing the animals you came for than one.

Around the park:

- **Tissamaharama**, the main base, has a large ancient reservoir and a restored stupa.
- **Kataragama**, about half an hour north, is a pilgrimage town sacred to Buddhists, Hindus and Muslims. Its evening ceremonies are busiest during the annual festival, around June to August.
- **Bundala National Park**, to the west, is a coastal wetland known for waterbirds, including flamingos in some years.

## Getting there and moving on

- **From the airport.** About 290 km, usually 4–5 hours by the Southern Expressway and the coast road.
- **From Mattala.** The southern international airport is about 30 km from Tissamaharama, under an hour by road, but it has few flights.
- **Into the hills.** [Ella](/places/ella/) is about 2.5–3 hours north via Wellawaya.
- **Along the coast.** [Mirissa](/places/mirissa/) is about 2.5–3 hours west, and [Arugam Bay](/places/arugam-bay/) about 3–4 hours north-east.

In a private tour you add Yala to your route on the map, and your driver gets you to the park. The drive times shown in the planner are rough estimates, not road-tested figures.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will I see a leopard in Yala?

Possibly. Block I has one of the highest leopard densities recorded, and many drives see one, but they are wild animals and sightings are never guaranteed. Two or more drives improve your chances.

### Is a morning or an afternoon safari better?

Both work. Mornings are cooler, with softer light and animals on the move. Afternoons build towards dusk, when activity picks up again. If you can, do one of each.

### When is Yala closed?

Blocks I and II usually close for several weeks around September and October, during the driest weeks of the year. Dates change annually. In 2026 they were 14 September to 15 October, and other zones stayed open.

### Should I choose Block I or another zone?

Block I gives you the best odds of seeing a leopard, along with the most jeeps. The other zones are quieter and wilder, but animals there are harder to find. Some visitors do one drive in each.

### Is a full-day safari worth it?

For keen photographers and birdwatchers, yes. For most people, two half-day drives are more comfortable, because the middle of the day is hot and many animals rest.

### Is Yala suitable for children?

Older children usually enjoy it. Drives are bumpy, dusty and hot, and you must stay in the jeep, so a half-day drive suits younger children better than a full day.
