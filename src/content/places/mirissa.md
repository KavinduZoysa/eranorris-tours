## Why stay in Mirissa

Mirissa is a fishing village on the south coast, about 35 km east of Galle, where a short curve of sand and palms faces south across the Indian Ocean. Around the headland to the west lies the fisheries harbour, from which the whale-watching boats leave in season. The village itself is small and mostly made up of guesthouses, restaurants and surf schools along the main road and the beach.

It is busiest from about December to March. In the evenings, the beach restaurants set out tables on the sand. During the south-west monsoon the village is much quieter.

## The beach and headlands

- **The main beach.** A sheltered crescent, usually calm enough for swimming in season, with shade from the palms behind it.
- **Parrot Rock.** A small rocky outcrop just off the beach, which you reach by wading across at low tide; a short climb gives a view along the bay. Don’t cross at high tide or when waves are breaking over the channel.
- **Coconut Tree Hill.** A headland of reddish cliffs and leaning palms a short walk from the main beach, crowded with photographers around sunrise and sunset. It is on private land, and access has been disputed in the past, so arrangements can change.
- **Secret Beach.** A small cove on the western side, reached from Harbour Road and a steep final path on foot. It is quieter, but has fewer facilities.

## Whale watching

From about November to April, boats leave the harbour around dawn and head for the deep water off the south coast, where blue whales, sperm whales and spinner dolphins feed. Most trips take several hours, and sightings are common in season but never guaranteed. Under the 2012 sea-mammal regulations, boats must be licensed by the wildlife department, carry a registered guide and stay at least 100 m from whales. The sea can be lively even in good months, so plan this for a morning when you have nothing else on. For the species, what a trip involves, the rules and seasickness tips, see the [Mirissa whale-watching guide](/experiences/mirissa-whale-watching/).

## Sea conditions and swimming

The south coast has two very different seasons:

- **About November to April.** The north-east monsoon blows offshore, and the bay is usually at its calmest. This is the season for swimming, snorkelling around the rocks and boat trips. Waves and currents still pick up near the headlands and rocks.
- **About May to September.** The south-west monsoon brings heavy swell, rain squalls and strong currents that can form quickly. The Sri Lanka Coast Guard, which runs a lifesaving post at Mirissa, has rescued several swimmers here caught in currents during this season. It asks beachgoers on the southern and western coasts to take particular care.
- **October.** A changeable month between the two, often with afternoon storms.

Wherever you swim, stay near other bathers and any lifeguards, respect red flags, keep away from rocks and river mouths, and ask locally if you are unsure. Turtles are sometimes seen near the rocks; watch them from a distance and don’t touch or feed them.

## Along the coast

- **Weligama**, about 5 km west, has a long, sandy bay with gentle waves and many surf schools, one of the most popular places in Sri Lanka to learn to surf. Lessons run mainly in the calmer months, from about November to April. Taprobane, the tiny wooded island off the beach, is a private villa.
- **Koggala and Ahangama**, about half an hour west, are known for stilt fishermen, though many now pose for paid photographs; the [Galle guide](/places/galle/) covers them with Koggala Lake.
- **Matara**, about 20 minutes east at the mouth of the Nilwala River, keeps a Dutch fort and the small Star Fort of the 1760s.
- **Dondra Head**, about half an hour east beyond Matara, is the southernmost point of Sri Lanka. Its 49 m white lighthouse was completed in 1890.
- **Hiriketiya**, a small horseshoe bay near Dikwella, about 45 minutes to an hour east, is popular with surfers. Nearby, the Hummanaya blowhole at Kudawella throws up spray when the swell is big.
- **Tangalle**, about an hour east, has quieter beaches and a slower pace.
- **Galle** and Unawatuna are about an hour west. The fort makes a day trip, or a stop on the way to or from Colombo.

## How long to stay

**Two nights** give you one morning at sea and one on the beach. **Three nights** are more relaxed and leave a spare morning in case a boat trip is cancelled because of weather, which happens even in season.

A typical three days:

- **Day 1:** arrive in the afternoon, swim, and walk out to Coconut Tree Hill or Parrot Rock for sunset.
- **Day 2:** an early start for whale watching, back by late morning or midday, then a slow afternoon.
- **Day 3:** a surf lesson at Weligama, Matara and Dondra Head, or the drive to Galle Fort.

## Best time to visit

Mirissa is at its calmest and driest from about November to April, which is also the whale-watching season. The busiest weeks run from about December to March. From May to September the south-west monsoon brings showers, rough seas and red flags, and most whale-watching trips stop. [Trincomalee](/places/trincomalee/) on the east coast has its own whale-watching season in roughly the opposite months. October and November, between the monsoons, can be wet.

## Getting there and moving on

- **From the airport.** About 175 km, usually 2.5–3 hours by the expressways, depending on traffic around Colombo.
- **From Colombo.** About 2–2.5 hours by road. The coastal railway also stops near Mirissa, roughly 3 hours from Colombo Fort, with only a few trains a day.
- **West to Galle.** [Galle](/places/galle/) is about 45 minutes to an hour on the coast road. [Hikkaduwa](/places/hikkaduwa/) is about another 30–45 minutes beyond.
- **East and inland.** [Yala](/places/yala/) is about 2.5–3 hours and [Udawalawe](/places/udawalawe/) about 2–2.5 hours. [Ella](/places/ella/) is 3.5–4.5 hours, a long day that many people break at one of the parks.

In a private tour you add Mirissa to your route on the map, and your driver handles the roads. The drive times shown in the planner are rough estimates, not road-tested figures.

## Frequently asked questions

### When is the whale-watching season in Mirissa?

Roughly November to April, when the sea on the south coast is calmest. Few trips run during the south-west monsoon from about May to September. Sightings are frequent in season but never guaranteed.

### Is it safe to swim at Mirissa?

Usually, from about November to April, though currents still build near the rocks and headlands. From May to September the sea is often rough, with strong currents, so follow the flags and lifeguards and stay out when it looks unsettled.

### Can I walk to Parrot Rock?

Yes, at low tide, by wading a short channel from the beach. Check the tide first and don’t attempt it at high tide or when waves are breaking.

### Is Coconut Tree Hill open to visitors?

It is usually open to walk up, but it is on private land and access has been disputed in the past. Check locally before you go.

### Where can I learn to surf near Mirissa?

Weligama, about 5 km west, is the usual choice: a long sandy bay with gentle waves and plenty of surf schools. Lessons run mainly from about November to April; ask the school about the day’s conditions.

### How many nights should I spend in Mirissa?

Two or three. If whale watching is the main reason for coming, allow three nights so you have a second morning in case a trip is cancelled because of the weather.
